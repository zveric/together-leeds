02 / Everyday examples

Could something like this have happened?

These are fictional examples, not legal decisions. You can skip this section if it feels difficult to read.

Possible hate crime

Damage and racist abuse

Someone damages your front door and uses racist abuse while doing it.

What this could mean

The damage and hostility may make this a hate crime. Tell the police what happened and why you think prejudice was involved.

Possible hate incident

A hostile comment

Someone directs a prejudiced comment about disability at you. No criminal offence is identified after assessment.

What this could mean

This may be a hate incident. Whether police record it depends on context, risk and the recording rules. Some speech is lawful.

Possible discrimination

Refused a service

A service provider refuses to serve you because of your race.

What this could mean

This may be unlawful race discrimination, even if no crime occurred. An adviser can help you consider a complaint or legal options.

03 / The law, made simple

Your rights

The law has different routes for hate crime and discrimination. One incident can involve more than one route.

Equality Act 2010

This law protects people from unlawful discrimination, harassment and victimisation. It applies in areas such as work, education, housing and access to services.

The nine protected characteristics are:

  • Age
  • Disability
  • Gender reassignment
  • Marriage and civil partnership
  • Pregnancy and maternity
  • Race
  • Religion or belief
  • Sex
  • Sexual orientation

Protection varies by setting and characteristic. Services and employers must make reasonable adjustments for disabled people where the law requires it.

Hate crime law

The five recognised hate crime grounds are race, religion, disability, sexual orientation and transgender identity.

Crime and Disorder Act 1998, ss.28–32: defines racial or religious aggravation and creates aggravated forms of certain assaults, damage, public-order and harassment offences.

Sentencing Act 2020, s.66: courts must treat proved hostility on the five grounds as an aggravating factor and state this openly. Racial or religious aggravation is not counted again under s.66 for ss.29–32 offences.

Public Order Act 1986: covers certain threatening or abusive conduct and stirring up hatred.

Protection from Harassment Act 1997: covers harassment and stalking; a course of conduct generally requires at least two occasions.

R v Rogers [2007] UKHL 8: hostility towards people as “foreigners” can amount to racial aggravation. Nationality is covered, not just colour or ethnicity.

The police investigate. Prosecutors assess evidence. Courts decide guilt and sentence. A report does not guarantee a prosecution.

Speech, privacy & recording

Human Rights Act 1998, Articles 8 and 10 ECHR: protects private life and freedom of expression. These rights are qualified; offensive speech is not automatically criminal.

Police must assess the context, risk and recording rules for non-crime incidents. Recording an incident is different from proving a crime.

R (Miller) v College of Policing [2021] EWCA Civ 1926: the recording guidance then in force disproportionately interfered with free expression. Non-crime recording must respect Article 10; the ruling did not ban all recording.

The five hate crime grounds and the Equality Act’s nine characteristics are different lists.

Thinking about a discrimination complaint?

Get independent advice promptly. Legal time limits can be short, and making a complaint does not necessarily stop the clock.

04 / Reporting & next steps

You choose where to start.

You can get support without making a police report. You do not need to collect evidence before asking for help.

Talk to someone first

Stop Hate UK offers a 24-hour helpline for people affected by hate in Leeds. You can ask about support and reporting options.

Call 0800 138 1625

Report to the police

Call 101 for non-emergency reporting, or use the West Yorkshire Police website. Tell them if you think hostility was involved.

In immediate danger, call 999

Ask about discrimination

An independent adviser, such as Citizens Advice, can help you understand complaint routes and when legal advice may be needed.

Visit citizensadvice.org.uk

Visiting the Reginald Centre?

You can ask a member of staff where to find support. You do not need to explain everything at the reception desk. Ask whether you can speak somewhere more private.

You could say: “Something happened and I’m not sure what to do. Can you help me find advice?”

What might happen after a police report?

  1. 01

    A conversation

    Police may contact you to discuss what happened in person at a police station or your home address. They may ask where and when it happened and whether you are safe. Tell them how you can be contacted safely.

  2. 02

    An assessment

    Police may work to identify a suspect. They assess risk and whether an offence may have happened. Ask how the incident is being recorded and for a reference number.

  3. 03

    An explanation

    Police may speak to the suspect and advise them about their conduct. Ask what happens next, who to contact and what support is available. Not every report leads to an investigation or prosecution.

If it feels safe, keep a record.

Save dates, messages or screenshots you already have. Do not confront anyone, put yourself at risk or share identifying details publicly.
